### Understanding the SA Radio Landscape

**Public SABC Stations** are the highest-reach platforms in South Africa. Ukhozi FM (8.2 million listeners) is the largest radio station on the African continent by listenership. A single rotation on Ukhozi FM or Umhlobo Wenene FM reaches more ears than most independent artists will reach on Spotify in a year. SABC stations are SAMRO tracked — every play generates a performance royalty.

**Commercial stations** (5FM, Metro FM, 947, KFM, East Coast Radio, Jacaranda FM) have smaller audiences than the major SABC stations but carry significant industry credibility and are closely monitored by industry professionals, labels, and brands. A 5FM playlist add is a career milestone that opens doors.

**Community and specialist stations** (Bush Radio, Rage Radio) have smaller audiences but are often more accessible to emerging artists and more willing to support local independent music. These are your first targets.

### Radio Submission Protocol

**1. Build your Electronic Press Kit (EPK) before pitching.**
Every radio submission requires an EPK. At minimum, your EPK must contain:
- Artist biography (150 words and 400 words versions)
- High-resolution press photo (minimum 3000×3000px, 300dpi)
- Music streaming links (Spotify, Apple Music, Boomplay — platform-specific)
- Social media handles and follower counts
- Previous radio play credits (if any)
- Contact details for your manager or yourself

**2. Follow the Submission Format exactly.**
The Submission Format field in each station entry tells you precisely how that station wants to receive music. Some require MP3 via email. Some require WAV files. Some want a specific subject line format. Deviating from the stated format is the fastest way to be ignored.

**3. Pitch on the Best Day and Time.**
The Best Time to Pitch field is based on when music directors have dedicated time to review new submissions and are most accessible. Most radio pitches are sent Monday morning — which means Monday morning is when inboxes are most congested. Pitch Tuesday to Thursday, 09:00–11:00, to stand out.

**4. Write a genre-appropriate pitch email.**
Your pitch email should be:
- Three paragraphs maximum
- Paragraph 1: Who you are and what you sound like (genre, comparable artists)
- Paragraph 2: The specific track you are pitching and why it fits their playlist
- Paragraph 3: Your streaming stats, previous airplay, and call to action
- Never attach a full audio file to a cold email — include a streaming link

**5. Follow up once, professionally.**
If you receive no response within 2 weeks, send one follow-up email. Reference your original pitch. If there is no response after the follow-up, move to the next station on your list and return to this one with your next release.

### SAMRO Registration — Do This First

The SAMRO Tracked? field exists to flag which stations generate performance royalties. Every station marked YES pays SAMRO every time your song is broadcast. SAMRO then distributes those royalties to you — but only if you are registered.

**If you are not yet registered with SAMRO, this is the most urgent action in your entire command centre.** You are leaving money on the table every single time your music plays on public radio. Visit samro.org.za to register as a member and register your works. Registration is free and royalties are paid retroactively within your registration cycle.

### The SA Music Press Hierarchy

Understanding where each publication sits in the ecosystem helps you prioritise:

**Tier 1 — International credibility builders:**
OkayAfrica, Afropunk, Bubblegum Club. Coverage in these publications reaches international audiences and is recognised by international labels, booking agents, and festival programmers as a credibility signal. Difficult to place — pitch only with strong, culturally specific stories.

**Tier 2 — SA industry credibility:**
Rolling Stone SA, Trax Magazine, Native Mag, Drum Magazine, Sunday Times Lifestyle. These are the publications that SA industry professionals read. Coverage here is what labels, brands, and promoters check when researching an artist. Target after your first significant release or a strong streaming milestone.

**Tier 3 — Audience reach:**
Daily Sun, City Press, Tshisa Live, ZAlebs, Channel24. These publications reach mass SA audiences — particularly Daily Sun with its working-class readership. Coverage here drives actual streams and show ticket sales more directly than Tier 1 and 2 publications. Target when you have a story with broad mainstream appeal.

**Tier 4 — Platform-native editorial:**
Spotify for Artists Editorial, Boomplay Blog, Audiomack editorial. These are not traditional press — they are platform-driven features that are triggered by performance data (streams, playlist adds, listener growth). You do not pitch these in the traditional sense — you build your platform metrics and they come to you, or you use the editorial submission tools within each platform's artist dashboard.

### Press Pitch Protocol

**Lead with the story, not the music.**
Music journalists receive hundreds of pitches per week. The ones that get coverage have a story — not just a song. Your pitch should answer: Why is this artist releasing this music right now? What is the cultural, personal, or political context that makes this story worth telling?

**Write a personalised pitch for each publication.**
Do not send the same email to Rolling Stone SA and Bubblegum Club. Each publication has a distinct editorial identity. Your pitch should reference specific content they have published and explain why your story fits their editorial direction.

**Standard music press pitch structure:**
- Subject line: [Music Feature] — [Artist Name] — [Release Name] — [Date of Release]
- Paragraph 1: The story hook — the single most compelling angle of your story
- Paragraph 2: Artist context — who you are, your genre, your audience, your streaming numbers
- Paragraph 3: The ask — what type of coverage you are seeking (interview, review, feature, premiere)
- Attach: EPK, hi-res photo, and streaming link (never attach audio files)

**Timing your press pitches:**
Pitch 2–4 weeks before your release date for review coverage. Pitch 4–6 weeks before for feature coverage or interviews, which require more editorial lead time. A review pitched on release day is almost always too late.

### Understanding the Festival Booking Hierarchy

**Headliner slots** are booked directly by festival programmers, often through artist management or booking agents, 6–18 months in advance. These are not accessible to most emerging artists without industry representation.

**Mid-card and support slots** are where most independent artists enter the festival circuit. These slots often have shorter lead times (2–4 months) and some are filled through open applications. The Artist Fee Range field reflects the range from support slots to mid-card.

**New artist / emerging stages** are explicitly designed for artists without festival credits. Look at the Local Artist Slots? field — any festival with YES or a named emerging stage is your priority target as an independent artist building your live career.

### Festival Application Strategy

**Apply 6–12 months before the festival.**
The Application Deadline field reflects when applications are typically due. Most SA artists apply too late. A festival taking place in October will typically close applications by April or May. Set calendar reminders based on this data.

**What to include in a festival application:**

- Artist bio (tailored to the festival's ethos — do not send a generic bio)
- Full EPK with high-resolution photos
- Streaming links with your current monthly listener count
- Live performance video (essential — most festivals require proof of live performance)
- Technical rider (PA, monitors, backline requirements)
- Previous festival or live performance credits
- Social media handles with follower and engagement data

**Build a festival portfolio progressively.**
The industry logic is cumulative: community events → corporate events → small festivals → medium festivals → large festivals. Each credit makes the next application stronger. List all your previous live performances in your artist bio and EPK even if they are not festival credits — promoters want to know you can hold a stage.

### Understanding the SA Music Industry Structure

**Collecting Societies — Register Immediately**

These organisations collect royalties on your behalf and are independent of whether you have a label deal. Every artist, regardless of career stage, should be registered with all applicable collecting societies.

**SAMRO (South African Music Rights Organisation)**
Collects performance royalties when your music is played on radio, TV, in venues, and at events. Membership is free. Royalties are distributed quarterly. Visit samro.org.za.

**CAPASSO (Combined Artists Publishers & Authors Society)**
Collects mechanical royalties from the reproduction of your music (streaming, downloads, physical copies). CAPASSO distributes royalties to SAMRO members with publishing agreements. Understanding SAMRO vs CAPASSO is essential — SAMRO pays the songwriter royalty, CAPASSO pays the publisher royalty. If you own your publishing (most independent artists do), you need both.

**DALRO (Dramatic Artistic Literary Rights Organisation)**
Collects royalties for synchronisation (music used in film, TV, advertising) and print licensing. If your music has been used in a production, DALRO tracks and collects those fees.

**RiSA (Recording Industry of South Africa)**
The industry body representing record companies. Relevant for collective bargaining, industry advocacy, and certification (gold/platinum records are certified through RiSA's systems).

**Distributors vs Labels — Know the Difference**

**Digital Distributors** (DistroKid, TuneCore, Africori, Amuse, Octiive) get your music onto streaming platforms and collect your recording royalties on your behalf. They typically charge either an annual fee or a revenue share. They do not sign you — they distribute you. You retain all rights. This is how most independent artists should release music.

**Independent Labels** (Kalawa Jazmee, iQhawe Music, Mushroom Group, Vth Season) offer marketing support, industry relationships, and sometimes recording budgets. They typically take a portion of your recording royalties (30–50%) in exchange for these services. Deals vary significantly — always have a music industry lawyer review any label contract before signing.

**Major Labels** (Sony Music Africa, Universal Music South Africa, Warner Music Africa, Gallo Music Group) offer the largest marketing infrastructure, international reach, and industry relationships. They are extremely selective — most artists who eventually sign to a major have 1 million+ monthly Spotify listeners or a viral moment before the label approaches them. Focus on building your numbers. If you are ready, they will come.

**Music Publishers** (Sheer Music Publishing, Africori Publishing) represent your songwriting rights and seek synchronisation placements, licensing deals, and co-writing opportunities. A publishing deal can be valuable if you write for other artists or want your music placed in film, TV, and advertising.

### Approaching Labels and Publishers — The Realistic Protocol

Do not send unsolicited music to major labels. The How to Approach field in each entry is specific — follow it. The general principles are:

**Build your streaming metrics first.** Every label and publisher uses streaming data to assess an artist's commercial viability. 10,000 monthly Spotify listeners is a minimum threshold for most independent label conversations. 50,000+ gets major label attention.

**Get industry representation.** Labels prefer to deal with managers and booking agents. Having professional representation signals that you are serious and that your business affairs are in order. Before pitching labels, consider whether a management relationship would serve you better first.

**Let your music travel.** A viral TikTok video, a major playlist add, or strong radio rotation will generate inbound label interest. Outbound pitching to major labels from a position of no audience rarely produces results.

### The Streaming Playlist Hierarchy

**Spotify Editorial Playlists** are curated by Spotify's in-house editorial team and carry the most weight in terms of stream volume and algorithmic impact. The submission process is through Spotify for Artists (artists.spotify.com) — submit your unreleased track 7–28 days before release date. There is no guarantee of placement, but a single placement on New Music Friday (South Africa) with 185,000 followers can add 5,000–50,000 streams in the first week.

**Apple Music Editorial Playlists** are curated by Apple's editorial team. Submit through your distributor — most major distributors (DistroKid, TuneCore, Africori) have editorial submission processes with Apple. Timing is similar to Spotify — 2–4 weeks pre-release.

**Boomplay and Audiomack** are the dominant platforms for African audiences outside of South Africa. Boomplay has 100+ million users across Africa and is the primary platform for West African markets. If your music has pan-African appeal, pitching Boomplay editorial is high priority.

**Independent Curator Playlists** are managed by individual curators with dedicated followings. These can be pitched via SubmitHub (submithub.com) or Groover (groover.co) — both platforms charge a small fee per submission (typically $1–4 USD) and guarantee that the curator will listen and provide a response. This is one of the most reliable ways for independent artists to get playlist placements.

### Pre-Release Streaming Strategy

A professional streaming release follows this sequence:

**4+ weeks before release:**
- Confirm your track is distributed and has a UPC/ISRC code
- Submit to Spotify for Artists for editorial consideration
- Submit to Apple Music via your distributor's editorial pitch process
- Begin pitching Boomplay and Audiomack if relevant to your genre

**2–3 weeks before release:**
- Begin submitting to independent curators via SubmitHub or Groover
- Pitch directly to any curators whose contact is listed in the Submission Link / Contact field
- Prepare your social media pre-release campaign

**Release week:**
- Monitor your Spotify for Artists dashboard daily for playlist adds
- Engage with all comments and shares immediately — early engagement signals feed the algorithm
- Post across all platforms consistently in the first 48 hours

**Post-release (weeks 2–4):**
- Continue curator pitching with live streaming link (no longer pre-release)
- Monitor your Discover Weekly and Release Radar performance through Spotify for Artists
- Note which playlists drove the most streams and prioritise similar playlists for your next release

### Grant Application Strategy

**Apply 3–6 months before you need the money.**
Grant turnaround times are typically 3–6 months from application to decision to disbursement. If you need funding for a June recording session, your application needs to be submitted by December at the latest. Plan backwards from your creative calendar.

**What most music grants fund:**
- Recording costs (studio time, engineer fees, session musician fees)

- Touring and live performance costs (transport, accommodation, equipment hire)

**Combine funding sources.** You do not have to fund an album from one grant. A professional independent album release might combine: a NAC grant for recording costs + SAMRO Foundation funding for touring + a private label's production budget + your own streaming revenue. Build a funding map for each major project.

### Understanding Streaming Royalties in South Africa

Streaming royalties are calculated in USD and converted to ZAR at the prevailing exchange rate. The ZAR rate fluctuates with the exchange rate — the figures in the template are estimates based on approximate rates at time of publication. The USD rates are more stable references.

**Royalty rate context:**
- Spotify: $0.003–$0.005 per stream → approximately R0.054–R0.091 per stream
- Apple Music: $0.006–$0.008 per stream → approximately R0.108–R0.144 per stream
- Boomplay: rates vary, African market-specific
- YouTube: $0.0005–$0.003 per view (wide range based on ad revenue)

To earn R10,000/month from Spotify at average rates (R0.07/stream), you need approximately **143,000 streams per month**. This is achievable — but it requires either substantial audience size or significant playlist placement.

**Priority platforms by market:**
- South Africa: Spotify (dominant), Apple Music, YouTube
- West Africa: Boomplay, Audiomack
- East Africa: Boomplay, Audiomack
- Diaspora: Spotify, Apple Music, Tidal

## Database 9 — Music Technical Specs

### Purpose
Music Technical Specs is a reference database of professional audio and delivery standards — the minimum technical requirements that your music must meet for professional distribution, radio submission, and sync licensing. This database exists because technical errors are one of the most common and most avoidable reasons that independent artist releases are delayed or rejected.

### Field Reference

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| Name | Title | Specification name |
| Specification Category | Select | Audio Master — General / Streaming / Radio / Sync / Artwork / Metadata |
| Required Value / Standard | Text | The exact technical requirement |
| Notes / Why It Matters | Text | The professional context and consequences of getting this wrong |

### Critical Technical Standards

**Audio Master — Non-Negotiable**

Your release master must be WAV (.wav) or FLAC (.flac) format. MP3 is not acceptable for distribution masters. Distributors convert from WAV/FLAC — they cannot work in the other direction. Submitting an MP3 master to a distributor may result in audibly degraded audio on every streaming platform.

**Sample Rate and Bit Depth**
Minimum standard: 16-bit / 44.1kHz (CD quality). Preferred standard for new recordings: 24-bit / 48kHz or 24-bit / 96kHz. If your recording is done at a higher sample rate, deliver at that rate — do not downsample before submitting to your distributor. They handle the conversion.

**Loudness Normalisation**
All major streaming platforms normalise audio to a target loudness level (Spotify: -14 LUFS, Apple Music: -16 LUFS). Over-compressing your master to sound loud on streaming platforms is counterproductive — the platform will simply turn it down. A well-mastered track at -14 LUFS will sound better than an over-compressed track at -8 LUFS after normalisation.

**Artwork Specifications**
- Minimum: 3000×3000 pixels at 72 DPI
- Preferred: 3000×3000 pixels at 300 DPI
- Format: JPEG or PNG
- Colour space: RGB (not CMYK)
- No blurriness, pixelation, or text that violates platform policies (no URLs, no social handles in artwork)

**Metadata**
Every piece of metadata must be complete and accurate before distribution. Incomplete metadata results in delayed royalty collection and misattribution of streams. Required fields: Track Title, Artist Name, Album/EP Title, Genre, Release Date, ISRC code (your distributor issues this), Composer name(s), Songwriter name(s), Publisher (or "Self-Released"), Copyright year and holder.

Build your live career in stages, matching venue size to your current audience:

**Stage 1 — Open Mic and Small Club Nights (0–200 capacity)**
Develop stage presence, build local audience, and create live performance footage. Venues at this level rarely pay significant fees — the value is experience, footage, and building a local following who will follow you to larger venues.

**Stage 2 — Small Venues and Support Slots (200–500 capacity)**
Begin earning from live performance. Support slots for established artists are more valuable than headlining to 100 people — the audience exposure accelerates growth. Target: R500–R5,000 per show.

**Stage 3 — Theatre and Mid-Sized Venues (500–1,500 capacity)**
This tier requires a dedicated fanbase and advance ticket sales. Venues in this range (like Joburg Theatre Studio, Soweto Theatre) often co-promote — they provide the space and some marketing while you drive ticket sales. Target: R2,500–R15,000 per show.

**Stage 4 — Large Venues and Major Festival Stages (1,500+ capacity)**
This requires established management, a booking agent, and a proven draw. The venue and promoter take most of the financial risk; artist fees at this level are negotiated through representation.

### Technical Rider — What to Know

Your technical rider is the document specifying your technical requirements for a live performance. Every venue's Technical Specs (Summary) field tells you what is already available in the house. The gap between what you need and what the venue provides is what you must supply yourself or negotiate.

A basic technical rider for an emerging artist should include:
- PA system requirements (watts, coverage)
- Stage monitor requirements (number of monitors, sends)
- Microphone requirements (vocal mics, instrument mics)
- DI box requirements (for keyboard, bass, click track)
- Backline requirements (if you use a drummer — kick, snare, hi-hat, cymbals)
- Lighting requirements (basic stage wash minimum)
- Green room / hospitality requirements

Keep your rider realistic at your current career stage. A 5-page technical rider from an artist with 500 monthly listeners will not be taken seriously. A clean, realistic one-page rider from the same artist will.
